## Context

Once Meridian gained adoption, the work got harder. More usage brought more demand for customization. A growing ecosystem brought more contributors, more opinions, and more pressure. Without active stewardship, the system would have fragmented under its own weight.

Most design systems fail at this stage because they aren't governed well, even when they were built well.

## My role

I was the platform steward. I defined the boundaries as the architect and enforced them in real time. The work was unglamorous and high-impact. It came down to a hundred small decisions, each obvious in isolation, that together determined whether Meridian would still be coherent in two years.

## What I did

**Ownership and boundaries.** Meridian is centrally owned. Components follow shared standards and a unified ownership model. I actively prevented distributed ownership of components across teams, because mixing ownership models creates fragmentation and dependencies that compound forever.

**Platform fragmentation.** I pushed back on proposals to introduce externally-owned components or allow partial integration of other design systems. My position was simple and I held it consistently:

> We can&rsquo;t have a couple components owned by another team and the rest owned by us, it would fragment the system and add dependencies.

**Strategic alignment.** I redirected teams that wanted to move faster independently or proposed solutions that didn't scale. I made every decision with end users and system impact in mind, along with local needs.

**Technical direction.** I resisted pressure to adopt alternative frameworks without clear benefit, and I did not shift architectural direction reactively. I made decisions on purpose and kept them in line with long-term platform strategy.

**Adoption and standards.** I balanced two competing forces, team velocity and system integrity. I did it through clear communication of standards, design alignment via Figma and Storybook, and consistent reinforcement of Meridian as the single source of truth.

## Resistance

**Pressure for autonomy.** Teams wanted flexibility, custom solutions, and control over components. Each request was reasonable on its own. Saying yes to all of them would have broken the system.

**External influence.** Other orgs proposed shared components, alternative frameworks, and joint ownership. Each came with its own roadmap, priorities, and dependencies.

**Scaling.** More contributors, more use cases, more edge cases. The system needed governance discipline that scaled with it.

## How I handled it

I was clear and direct, with no room for ambiguity about what was allowed and what wasn't. I communicated decisions firmly and consistently. I anchored every choice in system health, scalability, maintainability, and long-term value. I encouraged collaboration broadly and held control centrally where it had to be.

## Outcome

- **Meridian integrity.** It is still cohesive, still consistent, still scalable.
- **No fragmentation at scale.** Multiple competing systems and conflicting component standards were the most likely failure mode. They didn't happen.
- **Speed and quality.** Teams move quickly and reuse components without breaking the system.
- **Platform trust.** Teams trust Meridian because it's stable, consistent, and well-governed. That trust was earned through years of holding the line, and it is what makes Chapter 6 possible. Expert Insights ships into an organization that already trusts the front-end platform to be stable.
- **Sustainability.** The system continues to evolve because the foundations are protected.
