## Context

Vanguard wanted to take specialist expertise and put it in a real-time AI product for advisors. This couldn't be a demo. It had to be a platform with a real rollout path, and it was treated as a regulated product from day one, with risk and governance as requirements. Regulated finance doesn't forgive ambiguity there.

## My role

I was the delivery lead, and the main voice on the product narrative and the delivery plan. I also set the long-term technical direction: what we needed to think about for scale, and where the platform should go. The product has its own technical lead. The architecture in detail and the code are his and the team's work, and I didn't write much of the code myself. About half of my work was what we were building (the platform thinking). The other half was how we'd land it (the responsible-AI story and getting governance lined up).

## What I argued for

### A fixed shape for an insight

I argued early that the team needed to define what an "insight" *is* as a repeatable structure. Once the shape is fixed, a team can evaluate it, design around it, ship it consistently, and measure quality against a definition. If the model output is the product surface, none of that works, and nobody trusts it.

I had to win that argument with people before it was a technical one. The team had to agree that probabilistic model output gets shaped into a stable contract before it touches an advisor.

### A platform

I pushed the team away from treating the prompt as the product. I wanted a real architecture, a real API, a real rollout plan, and governance built in as a capability. It's the same approach as the design-system work in the earlier chapters: build the layer underneath first. The implementation is in the private architecture, so I can't show it here.

### Governance as a gate

I didn't treat responsible-AI work as something to add after launch. Governance and risk readiness gate the rollout. I made that dependency visible high enough in the org that it got staffed and resourced ahead of the launch window.

### The responsible-AI story for the board

I wrote the board-ready narrative for responsible AI and for the use case. The framing the team carried forward was that this isn't "running a calculation through ChatGPT." It's a system designed inside the firm's guardrails, with quality measured by the firm's own standards. The audience might know nothing about AI, and the story still had to keep the technical credibility that made it trustworthy. That translation mattered as much as the technology.

### One source of truth

I pulled the architecture, the program narrative, and the supporting briefings into one set of documents and got them into the right channels. After that, decisions moved at the speed of the program. Nobody had to hunt for a document.

## Constraints

**Governance and risk approval gated the rollout.** I pulled it forward into the plan so it never became the blocker.

**The board audience.** They might know nothing about AI, and the technical claims still had to hold. I used simple visuals, before-and-after framing, and careful control of depth, with the technical claims intact underneath.

**Proving quality for a probabilistic system.** "How good is good enough" has to be shown. The team built quality and confidence framing that the firm's own reviewers could evaluate. It wasn't a chart from a vendor.

## Outcome

- **Live for internal analysts since late 2025**, then piloted with advisors through the sales organization in spring 2026. The first pilot release shipped on schedule.
- **Publicly announced in pilot in April 2026.**
- Publicly it's framed as enhancing advisor judgment and not replacing it, which is what made the rollout last.
- Covered in Barron's, The Daily Upside, InvestmentNews, Citywire, ThinkAdvisor, Financial Advisor Magazine, PLANADVISER, and WealthManagement.com. See [the press section](/press) for the full list.

## What I would do differently

The broad advisor release did not land in the window I originally planned for. Product and methodology decisions that the next version depended on stayed open longer than the plan could absorb, and the date moved.

I had flagged those open decisions early. What I did not do was force them. Reporting a blocker is not the same as getting it resolved. A status line that says "waiting on a decision" can stay true for a quarter. What works is asking for a named owner and a date, and resetting the plan in the open the moment either one slips. I used exactly that on the analytics engine later in the year ([the analytics engine case study](/case-studies/09-analytics-engine-beneath-genai)), and it held. It belonged here a quarter sooner.
