## Context

Front-end at FAS was fragmented across apps, marketing sites, and content platforms. Teams were building inconsistent UI components. Designers and developers lacked a shared source of truth. Delivery speed was bottlenecked by rework, misalignment, and redundant implementations.

We needed a unified, scalable front-end system. The hard part was the cross-team alignment, the funding, and the sustained adoption work it would take to get there.

## My role

I was one of the founding engineers who defined, built, and scaled Meridian. I helped turn it into a platform with ownership, governance, and an adoption strategy. I co-authored the vision narrative, established front-end as a shared service model, and bridged engineering, UX, and business teams.

## What I did

**Vision.** I defined and promoted Meridian as a unified design system for FAS and framed it as a business enabler. The narrative positioned the system as a way to deliver consistent, on-brand experiences across product, marketing, and content.

**Components.** I built a centralized component library that serves AEM with about 40 components, Angular with about 30 apps and components, and Vue with about 35 apps and components. I designed it code- and framework-agnostic from day one so it would scale across whichever stack a team needed.

**Operating model.** I created a cross-functional model where Meridian is a shared service owned by FED and UX together. Design and development collaborate continuously. Marketing has direct access to the same components engineering uses, so marketing isn't blocked.

**Developer experience.** I established the developer experience and adoption channels. Storybook is the single source of truth, integrated with Figma. Developer workflows and onboarding run through the same documentation. Designers, engineers, and marketers share one mental model.

**Governance.** I rejected proposals that would introduce externally-owned components or split ownership models. Mixed ownership fragments delivery and creates dependencies, and I held that line consistently. Meridian remained a cohesive, centrally-governed system, and adoption aligned to one standard.

## Resistance

**Fragmentation pressure.** Teams wanted custom components, partial ownership, and integration of external systems. Allowing it would have killed the platform within a year.

**Cross-team misalignment.** Other teams operated at different speeds with different priorities. There was pressure to merge systems prematurely or adopt inconsistent practices.

**Behavior change.** Teams had to shift from building locally to using shared components, and from autonomy to platform alignment.

## How I handled it

I positioned Meridian as a business enabler. I combined flexibility, with multiple frameworks supported, and control, with central ownership and strict governance. I gave the same answer to the same question every time, so the platform's direction was never ambiguous.

## Outcome

- **Enterprise-scale adoption.** Meridian is used across 300+ pages on `advisors.vanguard.com` and multiple frameworks.
- **Standardized front-end development.** There is a single system for components, design patterns, and UI consistency.
- **Faster delivery.** Teams reuse components, build faster with less rework, and deliver consistent experiences.
- **Marketing and content.** Through the AEM integration that followed (Chapter 4), marketers and content creators use Meridian directly.
- **Platform foundation.** Meridian is now a front-end platform layer as well as a design system. It supports multi-framework apps, CMS integrations, and the AI work that came later.
